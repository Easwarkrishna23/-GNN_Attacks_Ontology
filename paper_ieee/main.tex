\\begin{abstract}
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have become a standard tool for learning from relational data, yet even small and localized perturbations to graph structure or node attributes can cause large drops in node classification performance. This report presents an end-to-end experimental study of adversarial attacks on two widely used architectures---Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) and Graph Attention Networks (GAT)---and evaluates defense mechanisms that improve robustness without sacrificing accuracy on clean graphs. We benchmark three poisoning attacks (random structure corruption, Nettack, and a meta-learning based attack) and three evasion attacks (edge flips, feature perturbations, and a gradient-based feature attack). Beyond standard defenses such as Laplacian feature smoothing, we propose an ontology-guided defense that uses semantic consistency between node features and class labels to reweight message passing and project features onto a more stable subspace. Experiments on the static Cora citation graph and a dynamic graph generator demonstrate that gradient-based feature manipulation is the most damaging evasion strategy in our setting, while the ontology-guided defense recovers a substantial portion of lost performance. We also provide layer-wise output visualizations, graph-before/after mosaics, and comprehensive metric tables to support interpretability of the attack impact.
\\end{abstract}

\\section{Introduction}
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) extend deep learning to non-Euclidean data by learning representations that explicitly depend on graph connectivity and neighborhood context. In practical deployments, however, the very mechanism that makes GNNs effective---message passing across edges---also creates an amplified attack surface: manipulating a small number of edges or a small fraction of feature entries can alter aggregated messages across multiple layers and degrade predictions far from the perturbation location. This is especially problematic in high-stakes settings where graphs are built from user-generated content, logs, transactions, or scraped data, and where the data pipeline can be influenced by adversaries.

This project focuses on node classification under adversarial manipulation. We pursue three goals. First, we implement and evaluate common GNN architectures (GCN~\\cite{kipf2017gcn} and GAT~\\cite{velickovic2018gat}) on a standard benchmark (Cora). Second, we compare attacks that occur during training (poisoning) to attacks that occur at inference time (evasion), spanning both structural and feature-based perturbations. Third, we design and test defenses, including a novel ontology-guided strategy that enforces semantic consistency between nodes and their neighborhoods.

The report is written in the style of a modern robustness paper: formal problem definition, explicit threat model, careful metric reporting, and evidence from both quantitative and qualitative diagnostics (confusion matrices, embedding plots, and layer-wise output inspections). While inspired by the broader literature on defending GNNs under graph perturbations~\\cite{zhang2020gnnguard,wu2019jaccard,entezari2020svd}, all exposition and experimental discussion are original and tailored to the code and artifacts produced in this repository.

\\section{Related Work}
Adversarial machine learning has historically focused on continuous domains such as images and audio, where perturbations are often bounded in an $\\ell_p$ norm. Graphs introduce different challenges: the structure is discrete, edges are sparse, and perturbations can have cascading effects through message passing. Nettack~\\cite{zugner2018nettack} and related methods explicitly search for edge and feature modifications that lead to misclassification, while meta-learning based approaches~\\cite{zugner2019metattack} optimize perturbations through bilevel objectives that reflect training dynamics.

Defenses for graph models often fall into two categories. Pre-processing defenses attempt to remove suspicious edges (e.g., similarity-based pruning) before training or inference~\\cite{wu2019jaccard}. Model-based defenses modify the architecture or training objective to reduce sensitivity to perturbations, such as robust latent representations or low-rank filtering of adjacency~\\cite{entezari2020svd}. GNNGuard~\\cite{zhang2020gnnguard} illustrates a hybrid direction: use feature similarity to estimate neighbor importance and suppress messages that appear inconsistent with graph semantics. Our ontology-guided defense follows the same high-level intuition---messages should align with semantic similarity---but is implemented as an explicit semantic matrix that reweights propagation and projects features toward a stable manifold.

\\section{Background}
\\subsection{Graphs and Node Classification}
Let $\\mathcal{G}=(\\mathcal{V},\\mathcal{E},\\mathbf{X})$ be a graph with $n=|\\mathcal{V}|$ nodes, edges $\\mathcal{E}$, and a node-feature matrix $\\mathbf{X}\\in\\mathbb{R}^{n\\times d}$. In node classification, each node $v\\in\\mathcal{V}$ is associated with a label $y_v\\in\\{1,\\dots,C\\}$. A model $f_\\theta(\\mathcal{G})$ produces class probabilities $\\mathbf{p}_v$ per node, and training typically minimizes cross-entropy loss over a labeled subset.

\\subsection{Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN)}
GCN~\\cite{kipf2017gcn} performs message passing through a normalized adjacency matrix. With $\\tilde{\\mathbf{A}}=\\mathbf{A}+\\mathbf{I}$ and $\\tilde{\\mathbf{D}}_{ii}=\\sum_j\\tilde{\\mathbf{A}}_{ij}$, one layer can be written as:
\\begin{equation}
\\mathbf{H}^{(\\ell+1)} = \\sigma\\left(\\tilde{\\mathbf{D}}^{-\\frac{1}{2}}\\tilde{\\mathbf{A}}\\tilde{\\mathbf{D}}^{-\\frac{1}{2}}\\mathbf{H}^{(\\ell)}\\mathbf{W}^{(\\ell)}\\right),
\\end{equation}
where $\\mathbf{H}^{(0)}=\\mathbf{X}$, $\\sigma(\\cdot)$ is a nonlinearity, and $\\mathbf{W}^{(\\ell)}$ are trainable weights. In our implementation, we use a standard two-layer GCN with ReLU and dropout.

\\subsection{Graph Attention Networks (GAT)}
GAT~\\cite{velickovic2018gat} replaces fixed normalization with learned attention weights. For node $i$ and neighbor $j$, attention coefficients take the form:
\\begin{equation}
\\alpha_{ij} = \\mathrm{softmax}_j\\left(\\mathrm{LeakyReLU}(\\mathbf{a}^\\top [\\mathbf{W}\\mathbf{h}_i\\,\\Vert\\,\\mathbf{W}\\mathbf{h}_j])\\right),
\\end{equation}
and the message passing aggregates neighbors using these coefficients (often with multi-head attention). This adaptivity can improve accuracy but may also create new pathways for adversarial influence if attention weights can be steered by perturbations.

\\section{Threat Model and Problem Formulation}
We consider two adversary capabilities.
\\textit{Poisoning attacks} modify training data---edges and/or features---before model training. The attacker aims to lower final test-time accuracy, either broadly (non-targeted) or on specific target nodes (targeted). Poisoning is realistic when graphs are collected over time and adversaries can inject interactions or falsify link evidence.
\\textit{Evasion attacks} modify the input presented at inference time while the trained model remains fixed. These attacks capture scenarios where the attacker can edit a user profile, manipulate a document feature vector, or flip a small number of connections at prediction time.

The attacker is budget-limited. We report budgets in a way that matches the implementation: number of edge perturbations, binary flips per node for feature attacks, and $\\epsilon$ for gradient-based feature manipulation. The defender is assumed to have access to training labels and can deploy defenses at training time (robust training or pre-processing) and/or inference time (feature denoising, reweighted message passing).

\\subsection{Datasets}
\\textbf{Static graph: Cora.} Cora is a citation network with sparse bag-of-words features and $7$ classes. It is a common benchmark for semi-supervised node classification, and its structure (citations) and features (keywords) create both homophily regions and noisy boundaries that are useful for robustness analysis.

\\textbf{Dynamic graph: evolving synthetic stream.} To evaluate robustness under non-stationarity, we implement a dynamic graph generator that evolves by adding nodes and edges over time. This setting captures distribution shift, temporal sparsity, and changing neighborhood composition, which can interact with attack and defense behavior in ways that static evaluations do not reveal.

\\subsection{Attack Suite}
We evaluate six attacks:
\\begin{itemize}
\\item Poisoning: Random Structure corruption (edge perturbations plus light feature corruption).
\\item Poisoning: Nettack-style targeted perturbations~\\cite{zugner2018nettack}.
\\item Poisoning: Meta-learning attack that optimizes perturbations through a bilevel objective~\\cite{zugner2019metattack}.
\\item Evasion: Edge Flip (localized degree-preserving structural manipulation around targets).
\\item Evasion: Feature perturbation (binary flips and continuous noise on selected nodes).
\\item Evasion: Gradient (FGSM-like) feature attack~\\cite{goodfellow2015fgsm}.
\\end{itemize}

While each attack differs in how it selects perturbations, they share a common principle: they seek to maximize loss or induce misclassification under a limited budget. Evasion attacks can be evaluated on a fixed trained model, whereas poisoning attacks require re-training on the modified graph to reflect realistic training-time corruption.

\\begin{algorithm}[t]
\\caption{Evaluation Procedure (Static or Dynamic Graph)}
\\label{alg:pipeline}
\\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\\STATE Load graph $\\mathcal{G}=(\\mathcal{V},\\mathcal{E},\\mathbf{X})$, labels $\\mathbf{y}$, and train/test masks.
\\STATE Train baseline model $f_\\theta$ (GCN or GAT) on clean data.
\\STATE Evaluate baseline metrics and store probabilities on the test set.
\\FOR{each attack $\\mathcal{A}$ in attack suite}
  \\STATE Generate attacked data $\\mathcal{G}_{\\mathcal{A}}$ under budget $b$.
  \\IF{$\\mathcal{A}$ is poisoning}
    \\STATE Train a new model on $\\mathcal{G}_{\\mathcal{A}}$.
  \\ENDIF
  \\STATE Evaluate attacked metrics and compute robustness metrics.
\\ENDFOR
\\STATE Select the most impactful attack (largest accuracy drop).
\\STATE Apply defenses (smoothing, ontology) and re-evaluate.
\\STATE Write CSV tables and visualization artifacts to `results/`.
\\end{algorithmic}
\\end{algorithm}

\\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We report standard classification metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and Macro-F1. We additionally report probabilistic metrics (ROC-AUC and log-loss) to capture changes in confidence calibration under attack. For robustness, we track an Attack Success Rate (ASR) derived from relative accuracy degradation, along with margin-based confidence changes. Structural diagnostics include density, modularity, and conductance computed with respect to label-induced communities.

\\section{Defense Mechanisms}
\\subsection{Baseline Defense: Laplacian Feature Smoothing}
As a simple and effective defense, we apply Laplacian smoothing to features prior to (re-)training. The intuition is that if the graph is largely homophilous, then nearby nodes should exhibit similar features; smoothing reduces high-frequency perturbations. A typical update is:
\\begin{equation}
\\mathbf{X}_{\\mathrm{smooth}} = \\alpha\\mathbf{X} + (1-\\alpha)\\hat{\\mathbf{A}}\\mathbf{X},
\\end{equation}
where $\\hat{\\mathbf{A}}$ is a normalized adjacency operator. We treat $\\alpha$ as a tunable parameter and report the best setting used in the produced results.

\\subsection{Ontology-Guided Defense}
The core idea of our ontology-guided defense is to encode a semantic prior over nodes and then use it to regularize message passing. In practice, we build an ontology matrix $\\mathbf{O}$ that captures feature-label affinity and feature co-occurrence patterns (a lightweight proxy for ``topic'' semantics). We then use $\\mathbf{O}$ in two complementary ways:
\\begin{enumerate}
\\item \\textbf{Adjacency reweighting:} suspicious edges that connect semantically unrelated nodes are down-weighted, reducing the influence of likely adversarial connections.
\\item \\textbf{Feature projection:} node features are projected toward a semantically consistent subspace, dampening adversarial feature edits that contradict the ontology.
\\end{enumerate}
Unlike purely structural defenses (e.g., low-rank filtering~\\cite{entezari2020svd}), this approach uses both features and labels to decide which information should propagate. This is particularly useful when the attacker targets features directly (as in gradient-based evasion).

\\begin{algorithm}[t]
\\caption{Ontology-Guided Defense (Feature Projection + Reweighted Aggregation)}
\\label{alg:ontology}
\\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\\STATE Input: adjacency $\\mathbf{A}$, features $\\mathbf{X}$, labels $\\mathbf{y}$ (training set), strength $\\lambda$.
\\STATE Build ontology matrix $\\mathbf{O}$ using feature--label affinity and co-occurrence statistics.
\\STATE Reweight edges: $\\mathbf{A}' \\leftarrow (1-\\lambda)\\mathbf{A} + \\lambda\\,\\phi(\\mathbf{O})$, where $\\phi$ maps semantic affinity to edge weights.
\\STATE Project features: $\\mathbf{X}' \\leftarrow (1-\\lambda)\\mathbf{X} + \\lambda\\,\\mathbf{O}\\mathbf{X}$.
\\STATE Train or fine-tune GNN on $(\\mathbf{A}',\\mathbf{X}')$ and evaluate.
\\end{algorithmic}
\\end{algorithm}

\\section{Experimental Setup}
We follow a standard train/validation/test split for Cora as produced by the dataset loader. Models use two layers, dropout, and Adam optimization. Poisoning attacks retrain the model on the perturbed graph to reflect the attack setting. Evasion attacks evaluate on the same trained weights. All random seeds are fixed for reproducibility in the provided code.

We emphasize transparency: each experiment run writes CSV metric tables and multiple figures into the `results/` directory, which are included in this report. This tight coupling between report and artifacts makes it easier to validate that the reported numbers correspond to actual executions.

\\subsection{Implementation Notes}
The implementation is modular so that attacks, defenses, and visualizations can be extended without rewriting the training loop. Data loaders live under `datasets/`, models under `models/`, attacks under `attacks/`, defenses under `defenses/`, and plotting utilities under `visualization/`. The experiment driver (in `main.py`) orchestrates the following outputs:
metric tables (CSV), graph mosaics, confusion matrices, t-SNE embedding plots, and layer-wise activation panels. These artifacts are stored under `results/` and are directly referenced by this report through the `\\texttt{\\textbackslash graphicspath}` configuration.

\\section{Discussion}
Three observations stand out.
\\textit{(1) Gradient-aligned feature attacks dominate.} When the attacker can access gradients (or approximate them), small perturbations in feature space can cause dramatic performance collapse. This indicates that robustness cannot rely solely on structural defenses.
\\textit{(2) Defense must preserve usefulness.} Over-aggressive filtering can remove informative edges and hurt clean accuracy. Our ontology-guided defense aims to be selective: it down-weights suspicious edges and projects features toward semantic consistency rather than hard-pruning large portions of the graph.
\\textit{(3) Interpretability artifacts help debugging.} Layer-wise outputs and embedding visualizations made it easier to confirm that the attack truly changes internal representations, and that defenses shift intermediate activations toward the clean regime.

\\section{Limitations and Future Work}
This work has limitations that motivate follow-up improvements. The ontology matrix currently uses a lightweight proxy derived from available features and labels; richer ontologies (e.g., knowledge-graph relations) may yield stronger defenses but require additional data sources. The current evaluation also focuses on node classification; extending to link prediction or graph classification would test generality. Finally, a tighter threat model for black-box attackers and a systematic hyperparameter sweep for defense settings would improve realism and reproducibility.

\\section{Conclusion}
We implemented a full robustness evaluation framework for GCN and GAT under multiple poisoning and evasion attacks, and we provided a defense pipeline combining feature smoothing with an ontology-guided semantic regularization strategy. On the static Cora benchmark, gradient-based feature attacks cause the largest degradation, and the ontology-based defense substantially restores accuracy and improves confidence calibration. Dynamic evaluations reveal similar vulnerabilities under evolving graph structure. The repository includes complete experimental artifacts---CSV metric tables, graph mosaics, confusion matrices, embedding plots, and layer-wise output panels---to support transparent reporting and further extensions.
